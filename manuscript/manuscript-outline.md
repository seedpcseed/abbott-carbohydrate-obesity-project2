# Manuscript outline and claim lock

**Project:** Abbott–Lurie ZC07, Project 2  
**Evidence source:** `render/integrated-scfa-microbiome.html` (rendered July 19, 2026)  
**Article concept:** Human ex vivo adolescent gut-microbiome functional-phenotyping study

## Editorial position

Frame this as a paired ex vivo fermentation study, not an efficacy study, a test of human carbohydrate digestion, or a direct replication of the originating animal work. The experiment measures how fecal microbial communities respond when directly exposed to carbohydrate formulations outside the host. It therefore supports claims about microbial SCFA accumulation, community response, and interpersonal heterogeneity—not glycemic benefit, obesity treatment, colonic delivery in vivo, or host health.

The strongest paper is led by three observations:

1. Incubation time and carbohydrate condition shaped analyte-specific SCFA trajectories, but adjusted pairwise contrasts did not show SDC superiority over RDC for acetate, propionate, or butyrate.
2. Donors showed substantial and repeatable high-to-low SCFA response ordering across RDC and SDC.
3. None of the six prespecified obesity-group SCFA difference-in-change contrasts was significant, but the confidence intervals do not establish equivalence.

The microbiome results refine rather than replace that story. Time and obesity group were associated with Bray–Curtis community structure; carbohydrate and group-by-carbohydrate terms were not significant in the donor-aggregated PERMANOVA. The head-to-head 48-hour relative-abundance analysis identified *Fusicatenibacter* as higher under SDC than RDC at exploratory q<0.1, but qPCR-scaled analyses did not establish a significant SDC expansion, and the donor-level *Fusicatenibacter*–butyrate association was positive but nonsignificant.

## One-sentence paper message

> Ex vivo SCFA accumulation by adolescent fecal microbiota varied with incubation time, carbohydrate condition, and donor identity, whereas obesity status did not significantly modify SCFA changes; exploratory microbiome analyses identified a relative *Fusicatenibacter* signal under SDC that was not supported as a significant quantitative taxon–butyrate association.

## Abstract scaffold

Draft the final Abstract only after the assay unit, sample exclusions, and render inconsistencies are resolved.

### Background

Carbohydrate structure may influence microbial fermentation, but ex vivo responses can vary between individuals. It is uncertain whether obesity status or donor-level microbial characteristics explain these responses in adolescents.

### Methods

State that fecal communities from 16 analyzed donors—8 healthy-weight and 8 with obesity—were exposed to no added carbohydrate, RDC, and SDC. Describe the 0- and 48-hour measurements; PFBBr negative-chemical-ionization GC-MS; 16S rRNA sequencing; total bacterial 16S qPCR scaling; the donor → A/B aliquot → culture-well hierarchy; and donor-aware mixed models.

### Results

Report, in this order:

1. Time effects and the adjusted carbohydrate change contrasts for acetate, propionate, and butyrate.
2. The absence of significant SDC-versus-RDC differences for all three primary analytes, including the prespecified butyrate result: mean change 7.95 μM under SDC versus 6.92 μM under RDC; adjusted difference 1.03 μM (95% CI −0.85 to 2.91; p=0.396).
3. The six obesity-group estimates and the statement that equivalence was not evaluated.
4. ICCs and RDC–SDC rank correlations as the quantitative interpersonal-variation result.
5. The donor-aggregated PERMANOVA: strong time effect, modest group effect, and nonsignificant carbohydrate and group-by-carbohydrate terms.
6. The exploratory relative *Fusicatenibacter* result, followed immediately by the nonsignificant donor-level taxon–butyrate association.

### Conclusion

> Ex vivo SCFA accumulation varied by incubation time, carbohydrate condition, and donor. Obesity status did not significantly modify SCFA changes, but equivalence was not established. Exploratory taxon-level findings require quantitative and mechanistic validation.

## 1. Introduction

Use a four- or five-paragraph funnel.

### Paragraph 1: Pediatric obesity and response heterogeneity

Establish the clinical context briefly. Focus on heterogeneity in metabolic response rather than cataloging obesity-associated microbiome signatures.

### Paragraph 2: Microbial carbohydrate fermentation as a functional phenotype

Explain how nondigested carbohydrates are converted to acetate, propionate, butyrate, and other metabolites. State that culture concentrations reflect net accumulation—production minus consumption or conversion—not flux or host exposure.

### Paragraph 3: Carbohydrate formulation and the rodent anchor study

Describe the preceding rat work as biological rationale. The 2022 study replaced a rapidly digestible formulation within a high-fat diet with a multi-component slower-digesting blend and reported microbiome changes and higher fecal acetate and propionate. Distinguish its in vivo dietary replacement from this study’s direct ex vivo microbial exposure. ([Plaza-Díaz et al., 2022][1])

### Paragraph 4: Translational gap

State that animal experiments do not establish how adolescent human microbial communities differ in their intrinsic ex vivo fermentation response. Avoid implying that expected host digestibility was measured in the culture system.

### Paragraph 5: Objective and hypotheses

Suggested objective:

> We characterized donor-specific SCFA and microbial community responses of adolescent fecal microbiota to rapid- and slow-digesting carbohydrate formulations ex vivo and evaluated whether these responses differed by obesity status or were associated with microbial community features.

Primary hypotheses:

1. SCFA trajectories would vary across no-added-carbohydrate, RDC, and SDC conditions.
2. **SDC would produce a greater 0-to-48-hour butyrate increase than RDC** (prespecified carbohydrate contrast; estimand `(SDC 48H − SDC 0H) − (RDC 48H − RDC 0H)`).
3. Obesity status would modify carbohydrate-specific SCFA change.
4. SCFA responses would vary among donors.

Exploratory objectives:

- test community-level associations with time, carbohydrate, and obesity group;
- identify carbohydrate-associated genera using relative and qPCR-scaled frameworks;
- evaluate continuous, donor-level taxon–SCFA relationships; and
- describe median-defined response strata without treating them as validated phenotypes.

Do not add a mechanistic *Fusicatenibacter* (or “SDC-utilizing butyrate producer”) hypothesis unless that mechanism was genuinely prespecified before analysis. The SDC-versus-RDC butyrate contrast itself is already a primary hypothesis above; report the test as hypothesis-driven, and state the nonsignificant result without reframing the claim as exploratory after the fact.

## 4. Discussion

### 4.1 Principal findings

Open with four findings:

1. SCFAs accumulated during culture, and carbohydrate condition modified trajectories in an analyte-specific manner.
2. SDC did not differ significantly from RDC for change in any primary SCFA.
3. Obesity-group SCFA differences were not significant, but equivalence was not established.
4. Donor response ordering was strongly repeatable across active carbohydrates.

Then state that time and obesity group, rather than carbohydrate, explained the claim-facing whole-community variation, and describe *Fusicatenibacter* as exploratory.

### 4.2 Relationship to the originating animal work

The rat study incorporated formulations into complete diets, allowing host digestion to determine colonic exposure and permitting host metabolic outcomes. This study bypassed digestion and directly exposed fecal communities. It isolates ex vivo microbial response potential but cannot demonstrate in vivo delivery or clinical benefit.

### 4.3 Interpreting carbohydrate effects

Discuss why all conditions accumulated SCFAs, including background fermentation in the no-added-carbohydrate medium. The significant negative RDC-versus-control propionate contrast and otherwise nonsignificant pairwise results argue against a simple “more substrate equals more SCFA” narrative. Consider medium background carbohydrate, substrate composition, pH, cross-feeding, and net metabolite consumption as possible explanations, clearly labeled as hypotheses.

### 4.4 Obesity-group results

Explain the absence of significant SCFA effect modification without claiming preserved capacity. Note the small sample—8 donors per group—and the width of the acetate intervals. Contrast the null metabolic interaction with the modest group-associated Bray–Curtis signal: composition and measured fermentation output need not show the same group pattern.

### 4.5 Interpersonal response heterogeneity

Make this the conceptual center of the Discussion. High ICCs and RDC–SDC correlations imply generalized donor fermentative responsiveness. This supports testing ex vivo response as a continuous phenotype in future work, but it does not validate a substrate-specific classifier or dietary-response predictor.

### 4.6 *Fusicatenibacter* as a hypothesis, not a mechanism

Discuss the genus as a relative marker distinguishing SDC from RDC at 48 hours. Acknowledge:

- the qPCR-scaled SDC interaction was nonsignificant after multiplicity;
- the donor-level taxon–butyrate interval included zero;
- relative abundance can rise without absolute expansion;
- concurrent change does not establish direction of causation;
- 16S data do not establish strain function; and
- substrate use and metabolite flux were not measured.

Mechanistic follow-up should include isolate growth on the exact formulations, substrate disappearance, metabolite profiles, co-culture, and ideally isotope tracing.

### 4.7 Strengths

- Paired exposure of each donor’s community to three conditions.
- Correct donor → aliquot → culture-well hierarchy.
- Integration of targeted metabolites, 16S profiles, and total bacterial qPCR.
- Direct adjusted contrasts with uncertainty.
- Quantitative analysis of donor repeatability.
- Adolescent human samples.

### 4.8 Limitations

- Ex vivo system lacks digestion, absorption, transit, mucosa, immune signaling, and dietary context.
- Expected host digestibility is a formulation property, not something measured in culture.
- SCFA concentrations reflect net accumulation, not production flux.
- Only 0 and 48 hours were analyzed; 48 hours entails strong culture adaptation.
- Sixteen donors, eight per group, with unresolved exclusions from the planned cohort.
- SCFA concentration unit, dilution mapping, CV, and LOQ still require facility confirmation.
- Non-normal or heteroscedastic SCFA residuals.
- 16S genus-level and compositional limitations.
- qPCR-scaled estimates inherit uncertainty from relative abundance and total-load measurement.
- Limited agreement among differential-abundance frameworks.
- Concurrent taxon and metabolite changes limit causal and predictive interpretation.
- Exploratory q<0.1 threshold and multiple analysis families.
- Median dichotomization and sensitivity to A/B aliquot variation.
- No in vivo or clinical metabolic outcome.

### 4.9 Conclusion

Suggested text:

> Ex vivo SCFA accumulation by adolescent fecal microbiota varied with incubation time, carbohydrate condition, and donor identity. Adjusted changes under SDC did not differ significantly from RDC for acetate, propionate, or butyrate, and obesity status did not significantly modify SCFA changes in this cohort, although equivalence was not established. Community and taxon analyses generated testable hypotheses, including a relative *Fusicatenibacter* signal under SDC, but quantitative association, mechanism, and in vivo relevance require independent validation.
