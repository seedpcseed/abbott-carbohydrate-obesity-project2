## Prior-claim bullets (each mapped to citation numbers)

1. **Child and adolescent obesity has risen in most of the world over three decades and is projected to keep rising.** Combined thinness+obesity prevalence increased among girls in 140 and boys in 137 countries between 1990 and 2022, driven by obesity; overweight+obesity doubled and obesity alone tripled between 1990 and 2021. → **[1, 2]**

2. **The affected population is very large in absolute terms.** By 2021, 93.1 million individuals aged 5–14 y and 80.6 million aged 15–24 y had obesity; forecasts project 186 million aged 5–14 y with obesity by 2050. → **[2]**

3. **Adolescent obesity carries an early and substantial cardiometabolic comorbidity burden.** Between 67% and 86% of children with severe obesity have at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, spanning hypertension, dyslipidemia, dysglycemia, and steatotic liver disease. → **[5, 4, 6]**

4. **Youth-onset metabolic disease is more aggressive than adult-onset disease.** Type 2 diabetes diagnosed in adolescence carries higher morbidity and mortality than later-onset disease, and childhood obesity is associated with roughly threefold premature mortality before age 30. → **[3]**

5. **Metabolic and weight outcomes in adolescents are strikingly heterogeneous, within every intervention modality.** Across pooled lifestyle, pharmacotherapy, and bariatric cohorts of adolescents with severe obesity, BMI change spanned −50.2% to +12.9%, with comparably wide variation in cardiometabolic risk-factor response. → **[8]**

6. **This heterogeneity is not explained away by adherence or demographics.** Differences in weight-loss response persist after controlling for diet adherence, physical activity, sex, age, and medication, implicating genetic, neurohormonal, thermogenic, and microbial contributions. → **[9]**

7. **Individuals differ markedly in their metabolic response to identical dietary inputs.** Population coefficients of variation for postprandial responses to identical meals reach 103% (triglyceride), 68% (glucose), and 59% (insulin), and person-specific factors including the gut microbiome contribute more than meal macronutrient composition for some responses. → **[12, 13]**

8. **Between-person variation therefore constrains what average-effect nutrition trials can conclude**, and quantifying it requires designs and statistics built for that purpose rather than post hoc responder splits. → **[10, 14]**

9. **Host metabolic phenotype and microbial community phenotype are distinct constructs measured on different substrates.** Host phenotype is the individual's own physiological response — adiposity change, insulin sensitivity, postprandial glycemia. Microbial community phenotype is the fermentative capability of the community itself — which substrates it degrades and which metabolites it accumulates — and is measurable independently of the host. → **[14, 15, 18]**

10. **Microbial community output is substrate-driven and partly redundant across compositionally different communities.** Across donors with markedly different community composition, SCFA production from defined non-digestible carbohydrates was reproducible, indicating functional redundancy; substrate identity and pH, not donor taxonomy alone, shaped the acetate/propionate/butyrate balance. → **[15]**

11. **Yet SCFA-specific variability persists and is not uniform across the three acids.** Fiber interventions consistently raise total SCFA and acetate, whereas propionate and butyrate responses vary substantially between individuals — i.e., heterogeneity is acid-specific. → **[16, 17]**

12. **The adolescent gut microbiota is a distinct developmental state**, compositionally different from both children and adults and still maturing, which motivates studying adolescent donors rather than extrapolating from adult communities. → **[23]**

13. **Whether the microbiome causes pediatric obesity or reflects it remains unresolved (contested).** Causal evidence rests largely on gnotobiotic mouse transfer experiments; human causal data are scarce, cross-sectional pediatric findings are heterogeneous with inconsistent F/B associations, and pooled obesity–microbiome associations are small relative to inter-personal variation. → **[19, 20, 21, 22]**

*(13 bullets; drop #7 or #11 to land at 12, or drop #4 and #11 for a tighter 11.)*

## Conflicts / nuance

**Functional redundancy vs. individualized SCFA output — the central tension.** Reference **[15]** found SCFA production "surprisingly reproducible" across donors despite compositional divergence, while **[16]** and **[17]** emphasize individualized propionate and butyrate responses. These are reconcilable: redundancy is strongest for total SCFA and acetate; divergence concentrates in propionate and butyrate. This is precisely the axis our acetate/propionate/butyrate endpoints sit on, and the Introduction should present it as an open empirical question rather than a settled fact in either direction. Do not cite **[15]** as evidence that donors are interchangeable, nor **[16]/[17]** as evidence that donor identity dominates.

**Cause vs. correlate.** **[21]** (mouse gnotobiotic transfer) is the strongest causal evidence and is frequently cited as if it settled the human question; **[20]** explicitly states human causal data are scarce. **[19]** further shows the human obesity–microbiome association is small (≈2% diversity difference) and poorly predictive (33–65% classification accuracy). Present as contested. Do not let **[21]** stand unqualified.

**Pediatric microbiome signatures are inconsistent.** **[22]** reviews 70 studies and reports heterogeneous findings with inconsistent F/B ratio associations. This is a reason to *avoid* a dysbiosis-list Introduction, and should be cited that way.

**Most heterogeneity evidence is from adults.** **[12]**, **[13]**, **[9]**, **[16]**, **[17]** are adult or mixed-age. Only **[8]** is adolescent-specific for treatment-response heterogeneity, and **[23]** for microbiota developmental distinctness. Do not imply the adult magnitudes have been demonstrated in adolescents — say the principle is established in adults and that adolescent-specific data are comparatively sparse (which is itself part of the rationale).

**Guideline citations carry implicature.** **[7]** (AAP CPG) and **[3]**'s weight-loss sections both frame obesity as a treatment target. Citing them heavily risks the reader inferring that this study bears on obesity treatment. Prefer prevalence and burden citations (**[1, 2, 5]**) for scene-setting.

**Reference [24] is rodent.** The originating rationale is a diet-induced-obesity rat study. It motivated the substrate comparison; it does not establish anything in humans.

## What we must not overclaim (given claim lock)

- **No implication that this study treats obesity or measures host benefit.** Nothing in the Introduction should suggest that the adolescent obesity burden described in **[1–6]** is addressed by our intervention. The burden paragraph sets up *why between-person variation in microbial carbohydrate handling is worth characterizing*, not a therapeutic premise. Avoid verbs like "improve," "reduce risk," "manage," or "treat" anywhere near our design.
- **No host glycemic claim.** Reference **[11]** (TODAY) and **[13]** (Zeevi) are glycemic-outcome papers. If cited, they must appear strictly as evidence that *host* responses vary between people — explicitly labeled as a different measurement class from our ex vivo SCFA endpoints. We measure no host glycemia. Safest option: cite **[12]** rather than **[13]/[11]**, and frame it as inter-individual variation in diet response generally.
- **No SDC-superiority setup.** Nothing in this paragraph may pre-frame slowly digestible carbohydrate as the favorable arm. Reference **[24]** must be described as a rodent study that motivated comparing carbohydrate digestion rates, not one that established a benefit direction. Its reported acetate/propionate findings in rats must not be forward-projected onto our human donors.
- **No obesity-group equivalence or "preserved capacity" framing.** Do not introduce language in paragraph 1 that anticipates the healthy-weight vs obesity comparison resolving in either direction. Claims 1–4 establish burden; claim 13 establishes that the microbial contribution is contested. Neither licenses a statement about group-level fermentative equivalence.
- **No responder phenotypes.** Reference **[10]** is included specifically to make the opposite point: apparent responder subgroups in nutrition and obesity research are frequently statistical artifacts. Use **[10]** as a methodological caution, never as license to define responders. Do not use "responder," "high responder," or median-split language.
- **No microbiome prediction of dietary response.** **[12]** shows the microbiome explains 7.1% of postprandial lipemia variance — a modest share — and **[19]** shows microbiome-based obesity classification is near chance. Describe the microbiome as *one contributor among several* to between-person variation, never as a predictive instrument.
- **⚠ Reference [17] contains a *Fusicatenibacter* result — do not use it for any *Fusicatenibacter* claim.** Bartsch et al. report that arabinoxylan increased *Fusicatenibacter* in Bacteroides-dominated participants at q = 0.18, an exploratory, non-significant-by-conventional-threshold signal in a 23-person trial with a different fiber and a different design from ours. It does **not** establish *Fusicatenibacter* as a slowly-digestible-carbohydrate utilizer, as a butyrate producer, or as expanding in absolute abundance. If **[17]** is cited at all, cite it only for the general finding that baseline community type modulates fiber-specific SCFA outcomes, and do not mention its taxon-level results.
- **No 48-hour restructuring framing.** Nothing in this citation set supports carbohydrate-driven community restructuring on a 48-hour horizon; **[15]** in fact reports compositional shifts alongside reproducible SCFA output, which cuts the other way.
