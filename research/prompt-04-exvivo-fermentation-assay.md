## Prior-claim bullets (mapped to citation numbers)

**Assay class is established and standardized**
- Anaerobic batch incubation of human fecal slurry in a buffered, oligotrophic, peptone-containing medium is an established and protocol-documented method for evaluating how a defined substrate is fermented by a human microbial community [2, 1].
- The method is conventionally applied to the *non-digested residue* of a food or formulation, with upstream simulated oral/gastric/small-intestinal digestion performed to an international consensus standard [3, 2].
- Batch incubations are the appropriate design when many substrates or many donors must be screened under directly comparable conditions; continuous/multistage systems trade throughput for physiological realism [1, 15].
- Reported design parameters across canonical human studies include strict anaerobiosis, controlled initial pH, high-density fecal inoculum, and serial or endpoint sampling over ~8–72 h [2, 6, 8, 7].

**Donors differ substantially in SCFA response to the same carbohydrate challenge**
- Under identical substrate exposure, human donors produce different quantities *and different proportions* of acetate, propionate, and butyrate; this is the defining observation of the microbial-metabolic-phenotype framing [5].
- Donor-specific fermentation has been reproduced with purified fibres [5, 11], with food-matrix carbohydrates after enzymatic pre-digestion [7], and with a panel of whole-food fibres over 72 h [8].
- **In adolescents specifically:** fecal microbiota from 17 children/adolescents with obesity (age 10–18) metabolized all tested prebiotics, but showed "significant interdonor variation in SCFA production" that 16S profiling associated with differences in baseline community composition [4].
- Substrate complexity and choice of SCFA endpoint both modulate how large the between-donor variance is — butyrate-related endpoints are consistently more donor-variable than total SCFA [13, 11].

**What the assay CAN support**
- Relative microbial response potential: whether a community, on direct exposure, converts a given carbohydrate into SCFAs, and in what ratio [1, 2, 5].
- Donor heterogeneity: quantification of between-individual differences in that conversion, since host and dietary confounders are removed and every donor sees an identical substrate dose [1, 5, 4].
- Formulation comparison under direct exposure: head-to-head ranking of substrates within the same inoculum, which is the internally-controlled comparison the design is built for [6, 8, 2].

**What the assay CANNOT support**
- Host digestion and absorption: the vessel contains no small-intestinal absorptive surface; upstream digestion is simulated statically and omits gastric emptying kinetics [1, 12, 15].
- Host SCFA exposure: only 36%/9%/2% of colonically delivered acetate/propionate/butyrate reaches the systemic circulation in humans, so accumulation in a closed vessel is not a proxy for delivered dose — least of all for butyrate [9].
- Glycemic or clinical outcomes: no host tissue, no endocrine axis, no absorption; the model explicitly excludes host effects by construction [1, 15].
- Mucosal or immune interaction: no epithelium, no mucus layer, no immune compartment [1, 15].
- Correspondence to the donor's in vivo state: in the same pediatric-obesity cohort, fecal SCFA concentration, ex vivo SCFA production capacity, and obesity markers did not positively correlate with one another [4].

**Culture-time adaptation — why a 24–48 h endpoint is a community-adaptation readout**
- Substrate consumption is front-loaded: the largest changes in pH, diversity, liberated monosaccharides, and SCFAs occur within the first 24 h [8]. A 48 h endpoint therefore integrates the primary-degradation phase *and* a subsequent period in which the community has restructured under its own end-products.
- Community composition in culture diverges systematically from the input feces: dominant fecal taxa lose dominance while previously undetected low-abundance taxa bloom, driven by feed composition and pH [10].
- Falling pH is not a passive by-product — it re-partitions flux between butyrate and propionate independently of who is in the community [6]. At a 48 h endpoint, pH drift is therefore part of what is being measured.
- Cross-feeding chains (e.g. lactate and 1,2-propanediol handoffs to secondary butyrate producers) mean late-timepoint butyrate is frequently a *secondary* product of an adapted consortium rather than direct hydrolysis of the added substrate [6].
- Residence/incubation time is itself a dominant driver of SCFA quantity and chain-length distribution, comparable in effect size to substrate identity [11].
- Time-dependence is donor-differential: in a two-donor bread comparison, acetate and propionate differences present at 8 h had largely equalized by 24 h, while butyrate differences persisted [7].

**Functional-phenotyping framing**
- Preferred term and preferred citation: *microbial metabolic phenotype* (MMP) [5]; supporting framing as "fermentation capacity/capability" [5, 7] and "response potential" [1].
- Framing the assay as phenotyping — measuring what a community *does* under a standard challenge — rather than as prediction is the defensible position, and is how the closest pediatric prior art positions itself [4, 5].

## Conflicts / nuance

1. **Functional redundancy is the principal challenge to a donor-heterogeneity narrative.** Reichardt et al. found that despite marked inter-individual compositional differences, SCFA production across 15 carbohydrates was "surprisingly reproducible" between donors [6]. This is in tension with the MMP heterogeneity result [5] and with donor-specific findings [7, 8, 4]. The reconciliation supported by the literature: redundancy is strongest for *total* SCFA and for simple purified substrates, whereas donor-specificity is strongest for *butyrate* and for complex/food-matrix substrates [13, 7, 11]. The manuscript should state this explicitly rather than cite only the heterogeneity side.

2. **pH is a confounder that mimics a community effect.** Initial and drifting pH shift butyrate/propionate stoichiometry independently of community composition [6]. Any donor difference in butyrate at 48 h has a partially abiotic explanation unless pH was controlled or measured.

3. **Pooling versus per-donor inocula is a genuine methodological fork.** Pooling improves reproducibility and reduces inter-batch CV [14], and is the standard recommendation when the substrate is the object of study. It is the wrong choice here — but the manuscript should say *why* it was rejected rather than leave it unaddressed.

4. **Static upstream digestion is a real limitation for an RDC-versus-SDC comparison.** The rate distinction between a rapid- and a slow-digesting carbohydrate is fundamentally a *kinetic, host-side* property. A static INFOGEST-style digestion produces an endpoint residue and cannot reproduce gradual gastric acidification and emptying [3, 12]. What reaches the inoculum is therefore a rate-collapsed proxy. This belongs in Discussion 4.7 as a stated limitation.

5. **Batch vessels accumulate end-products.** Unlike continuous systems, closed batch culture retains SCFAs and gas, which inhibits later fermentation and depresses pH — a recognized structural limitation of the batch format [1, 15].

6. **Pediatric evidence is thin — flag explicitly.** A targeted PubMed search combining children/adolescents + fecal microbiota + in vitro fermentation + SCFA returned only **two** records. Only one [4] is a genuine donor-heterogeneity ex vivo study in adolescents. The other [PMID 40895066, [10.7717/peerj.19894](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.19894), PeerJ 2025, n=39 children aged 6–15, FOS] is a single-substrate fermentation study framed around composition and biomarker discovery rather than donor-level phenotyping, and is a weaker fit. Essentially every canonical methods and heterogeneity citation available [5, 6, 7, 8, 11, 13] uses adult (or animal) inocula. **This is a genuine gap and a legitimate novelty claim for the present study — but it also means the assay's behaviour with adolescent inocula is not independently characterized.**

7. **Two supporting variance/pooling citations are non-human** [13, 14]. Use them for methodological reasoning about donor number and pooling, not as evidence about human fermentation biology, and label them as such.

## What we must not overclaim (given claim lock)

- **Do not** use any citation here to argue SDC outperforms RDC for acetate, propionate, or butyrate. These citations establish that substrates *can be compared* within an inoculum and that donors differ — never that a slow-digesting formulation yields more SCFA. Reichardt's functional-redundancy finding [6] actively cuts against a strong formulation-superiority claim.
- **Do not** use donor-heterogeneity citations to support "preserved fermentation capacity" or obesity-group equivalence. Heterogeneity findings [5, 4, 7, 8] are about *variation between individuals*, not about equivalence between clinical groups. An absence of group difference in this design is a null result under a small n, not demonstrated equivalence.
- **Do not** convert donor variation into "responder phenotypes." No cited study validated a responder/non-responder classification. Blecksmith describes some donors as "more responsive" descriptively among 5 donors [8]; Holmes reports interdonor variation without defining responder classes [4]. Median splits are not supported here.
- **Do not** claim the microbiome predicts dietary response. Gurry reports that *aspects* of MMP can be predicted from 16S composition in an adult ex vivo setting [5] — this is ex vivo self-prediction, not prediction of in vivo dietary response, and it must not be extended.
- **Do not** attach any glycemic, digestive, or obesity-treatment inference to SCFA accumulation. Boets [9] is the affirmative citation that this inference is invalid; use it defensively.
- **Do not** describe 48 h SCFA accumulation as "SCFA production available to the host." Say *net accumulation in vitro* [9, 1].
- **Do not** present 48 h community composition as the donor's microbiome. It is a culture-adapted community [10].
- No citation here speaks to *Fusicatenibacter* as an SDC utilizer or butyrate producer, and none should be recruited to that purpose.

## Downstream synthesis blocks

### Block A — Introduction para 4 (assay class)

**SECTION:** Introduction, para 4
**PRIOR CLAIM:** Anaerobic batch fermentation of human fecal microbiota is an established, protocol-standardized method for measuring how a defined carbohydrate substrate is metabolized by a human microbial community under directly comparable conditions.
**CITATIONS (lead):** [2] Pérez-Burillo 2021 Nat Protoc (10.1038/s41596-021-00537-x); [1] Isenring 2023 Microbiome Res Rep (10.20517/mrr.2022.15); [3] Minekus/INFOGEST 2014 Food Funct (10.1039/c3fo60702j)
**BACKUP:** [15] Li & Zhang 2022 (10.1021/acs.jafc.2c04238); [6] Reichardt 2017 (10.1038/ismej.2017.196)
**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** No single consensus exists on model selection and operating conditions across laboratories [1]; batch vessels accumulate end-products and acidify, unlike continuous systems [1, 15].
**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Anaerobic batch incubation of human fecal microbiota with a defined substrate is a standardized approach for characterizing the fermentative capacity of a microbial community, typically applied to the residue remaining after simulated upper-gastrointestinal digestion, and is well suited to comparing multiple substrates within a single donor's community under identical conditions."
**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** Supported.

### Block B — Introduction para 5 (donor heterogeneity + functional phenotyping)

**SECTION:** Introduction, para 5
**PRIOR CLAIM:** Human donors differ substantially in the amount and proportion of SCFAs their microbiota generate from an identical carbohydrate challenge; this between-person functional variation is the phenotype the assay measures.
**CITATIONS (lead):** [5] Gurry 2021 PLoS ONE (10.1371/journal.pone.0254004); [4] Holmes 2020 mBio (10.1128/mBio.00914-20); [7] Pirkola 2023 Sci Rep (10.1038/s41598-022-26847-y)
**BACKUP:** [8] Blecksmith 2025 Food Funct (10.1039/d5fo00947b); [11] Minnebo 2023 Microbiome (10.1186/s40168-023-01691-y)
**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** Reichardt et al. report substantial functional redundancy — reproducible SCFA output across donors despite compositional differences [6]. Heterogeneity is most consistently reported for butyrate and for complex substrates [13, 7].
**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Ex vivo studies have repeatedly shown that fecal microbiota from different individuals generate different quantities and proportions of short-chain fatty acids from the same carbohydrate input, a form of functional variation that has been described as an individual microbial metabolic phenotype; the extent of this variation depends on substrate complexity and on which short-chain fatty acid is considered, with total short-chain fatty acid output showing greater cross-donor consistency than butyrate."
**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** Supported. Must not be extended to responder classification or to prediction of in vivo dietary response.

### Block C — Introduction para 5 (pediatric precedent and gap)

**SECTION:** Introduction, para 5 (closing) / rationale
**PRIOR CLAIM:** Ex vivo fermentation has been applied to adolescent donors with obesity, but such studies are rare.
**CITATIONS (lead):** [4] Holmes 2020 mBio (10.1128/mBio.00914-20)
**BACKUP:** PMID 40895066 (10.7717/peerj.19894), children aged 6–15, FOS — single-substrate, composition-focused
**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** A targeted search returned only two pediatric ex vivo fermentation records; the methodological and heterogeneity literature is otherwise adult- or animal-derived. Holmes et al. also found no positive correlation between fecal SCFA concentration, ex vivo production capacity, and obesity markers [4].
**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Ex vivo fermentation has been applied to fecal microbiota from children and adolescents with obesity, where donor microbiota metabolized all tested prebiotic substrates but differed significantly from one another in short-chain fatty acid output; such pediatric studies remain uncommon, and the assay's behaviour with adolescent inocula is far less characterized than with adult inocula."
**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** Supported. Does not imply obesity-group equivalence or preserved capacity.

### Block D — Discussion 4.7 (translational gap — the core paragraph)

**SECTION:** Discussion 4.7
**PRIOR CLAIM:** The assay measures direct microbial response potential under controlled exposure and cannot address host digestion, absorption, glycemic response, mucosal or immune interaction, or clinical efficacy.
**CITATIONS (lead):** [9] Boets 2017 J Physiol (10.1113/JP272613); [1] Isenring 2023 (10.20517/mrr.2022.15); [12] Mulet-Cabero 2020 Food Funct (10.1039/c9fo01293a)
**BACKUP:** [15] Li & Zhang 2022 (10.1021/acs.jafc.2c04238); [4] Holmes 2020 (10.1128/mBio.00914-20)
**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** The rate distinction between rapid- and slow-digesting carbohydrate is a host-side kinetic property that a static upstream digestion cannot reproduce [3, 12] — so what reaches the inoculum is a rate-collapsed residue. Separately, systemic availability is lowest precisely for butyrate (2%) [9].
**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Because the incubation contains no absorptive epithelium, no mucosal or immune compartment, and no host endocrine axis, net short-chain fatty acid accumulation in vitro indexes microbial production potential rather than host exposure; in humans, only a minority of colonically delivered acetate and propionate and approximately 2% of butyrate reach the systemic circulation, so these measurements cannot be translated into glycemic, digestive, or clinical outcomes."
**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** Supported. This block is the primary guard against glycemic/clinical overreach.

### Block E — Discussion 4.8 (culture-time adaptation at the 48 h endpoint)

**SECTION:** Discussion 4.8
**PRIOR CLAIM:** A 24–48 h endpoint reflects community adaptation to culture conditions as well as utilization of the added substrate.
**CITATIONS (lead):** [10] Gnanasekaran 2021 mSystems (10.1128/mSystems.00232-21); [8] Blecksmith 2025 Food Funct (10.1039/d5fo00947b); [6] Reichardt 2017 ISME J (10.1038/ismej.2017.196)
**BACKUP:** [11] Minnebo 2023 Microbiome (10.1186/s40168-023-01691-y); [7] Pirkola 2023 Sci Rep (10.1038/s41598-022-26847-y)
**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** Falling pH re-partitions butyrate/propionate flux independently of community membership [6]; late butyrate is frequently a cross-feeding product rather than direct substrate hydrolysis [6]; donor individuality is nonetheless retained in culture rather than converging to a vessel-specific community [10].
**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Because most substrate consumption and pH change occur within the first 24 hours, a 48-hour endpoint integrates both primary substrate utilization and a subsequent phase in which the community restructures under accumulating end-products and declining pH; community composition at this endpoint should therefore be interpreted as a culture-adapted state rather than as a representation of the donor's in vivo microbiota."
**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** Supported. Directly constrains any claim that 48 h compositional shifts represent carbohydrate-driven in vivo restructuring.

### Block F — Discussion 4.8 (per-donor inocula design justification)

**SECTION:** Discussion 4.8 (methodological reflection)
**PRIOR CLAIM:** Using separate per-donor inocula rather than a pooled inoculum is required when between-donor variation is the object of measurement.
**CITATIONS (lead):** [14] Wang 2026 BMC Microbiol (10.1186/s12866-026-04731-3); [13] Bosch 2017 J Nutr Sci (10.1017/jns.2017.21)
**BACKUP:** [1] Isenring 2023 (10.20517/mrr.2022.15)
**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** Both supporting studies are non-human (porcine, feline) and must be labelled as methodological rather than biological evidence. Pooling is the conventional recommendation when the *substrate* is the object of study; required donor n depends on which SCFA endpoint is targeted, with butyrate endpoints requiring more donors [13].
**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Pooling fecal donors reduces inter-sample and inter-batch variation in fermentation outcomes and is the conventional choice when a substrate rather than a community is under study; because between-donor variation was itself the quantity of interest here, each donor's microbiota was incubated separately, at the cost of greater variance in any individual endpoint."
**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** Supported (methodological). Non-human sources must be identified as such.
