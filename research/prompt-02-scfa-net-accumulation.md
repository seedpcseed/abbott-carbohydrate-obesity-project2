## Prior-claim bullets (mapped to citation numbers)

**Core physiology — producing taxa and pathways**

- Nondigested carbohydrates reaching the large intestine are fermented anaerobically by the resident microbiota, with acetate, propionate and butyrate as the dominant fermentation acids. [7, 12, 15]
- Human colonic butyrate producers are phylogenetically diverse Gram-positive Bacillota, most abundantly relatives of *Eubacterium rectale*/*Roseburia* spp. and *Faecalibacterium prausnitzii*; in most, the terminal step is catalysed by butyryl-CoA:acetate CoA-transferase rather than butyrate kinase. [8, 9]
- Propionate is formed by three distinct routes with distinct phylogenetic distributions: the succinate pathway (dominant from hexose sugars; Bacteroidota and Negativicutes), the acrylate pathway (from lactate; restricted to a few Lachnospiraceae/Negativicutes), and the propanediol pathway (from deoxy sugars such as fucose and rhamnose). [10, 7]
- Butyrate and propionate capacity generally reside in different species, though a minority of taxa switch between butyrate and propionate depending on substrate. [10]

**Cross-feeding and butyrate as a secondary product**

- Butyrate formation in a mixed community is substantially a **secondary** fermentation step: many butyrate producers cannot degrade the primary polysaccharide and instead consume intermediates — lactate, succinate, 1,2-propanediol and acetate — released by primary degraders. [7, 11, 12]
- Cross-feeding operates by at least two distinct mechanisms: consumption of fermentation end-products (lactate, acetate), and cross-feeding of partial breakdown products of the complex substrate. [11]
- Lactate is produced by many gut taxa yet rarely accumulates in a healthy colon, because a small guild of lactate utilizers converts it onward to butyrate, propionate or acetate. The measured lactate pool is therefore a production–consumption balance. [12, 13]

**Net accumulation is not production**

- Several major butyrate producers show **net acetate consumption** while producing butyrate; others are net acetate producers. Measured acetate in a mixed community is therefore the arithmetic balance of simultaneous production and consumption by different members. [9, 7]
- Luminal and fecal SCFA concentrations do not reflect the rate of SCFA production and should not be used as measures of production or absorption. [1]
- In humans, plasma but not fecal SCFA concentrations tracked modelled SCFA production rates; tracer approaches detected changes in SCFA metabolism more sensitively than either fecal or plasma concentration alone. [3]
- Faecal metabolite concentrations are insufficient to characterise the dynamic (nutrikinetic) processes of SCFA turnover; there is insufficient evidence to treat changes in faecal metabolite concentration as markers of a functional benefit. [6]
- Quantitative understanding of SCFA biology is limited principally by the scarcity of **flux** measurements, as distinct from concentration measurements. [5]

**Colonic delivery and host systemic exposure**

- Systemic availability of colonically delivered acetate, propionate and butyrate in healthy humans was approximately 36%, 9% and 2% respectively; the majority was consumed locally or oxidised, with SCFAs excreted mainly via the breath as CO₂. [2]
- Molar SCFA ratios shift systematically between colonic contents, portal blood and hepatic vein, indicating preferential butyrate uptake by the colonic epithelium and propionate extraction by the liver — so luminal ratios do not translate to systemic ratios. [4]
- Interconversion of acetate to butyrate within the colon (≈24% of administered acetate) was **not** related to the butyrate-producing capacity measured in the corresponding faecal samples. [2]
- Diet and microbiome features predict fecal SCFA far better than plasma SCFA, indicating the two compartments are non-interchangeable readouts. [19]

**Host effects — benefits and caveats together**

- SCFAs act as energy substrates, ligands for FFAR2/FFAR3, and HDAC inhibitors, and are implicated in gut barrier, immune and metabolic regulation. [15, 16, 17]
- Evidence for metabolic benefit derives predominantly from animal and in vitro work; the quantitative importance of SCFAs in human energy and substrate metabolism remains incompletely established. [16, 17]
- Higher **fecal** SCFA concentrations have been associated with lower microbial diversity, greater gut permeability, obesity and hypertension in a large community cohort — plausibly reflecting less efficient absorption rather than greater benefit. Higher fecal SCFA is therefore not interpretable as a favourable phenotype. [18]

**Batch fermentation time scales**

- Static human faecal batch fermentation with SCFA/gas/pH sampling over a **48 h** window is an established convention in the fibre-fermentation literature. [20]
- Within a 48 h batch window, substrates with similar endpoint SCFA can follow very different kinetics — some ferment almost entirely in the first half, others extend into the second half — so a single endpoint value does not describe fermentation rate or timing. [20, 13]
- In a closed batch system, accumulating acid lowers pH, and pH shifts themselves alter community composition and the relative success of butyrate producers versus Bacteroidota — a system property of the assay, not solely of the substrate. [14]

## Conflicts / nuance

1. **Direction-of-benefit conflict for fecal SCFA.** The mechanistic literature frames SCFAs as beneficial [15, 16, 17], but the largest observational human dataset found higher *fecal* SCFA associated with dysbiosis, obesity and hypertension [18]. These are reconcilable only if fecal SCFA is read as an **excretion/absorption-efficiency** signal rather than a production signal. This is the strongest available argument for our "net accumulation" language, and it should be presented as a genuine interpretive tension, not resolved.

2. **Fecal vs. plasma compartments disagree.** Kirschner et al. found plasma but not fecal concentrations correlated with modelled production [3]; Oliver et al. found diet/microbiome predicted fecal far better than plasma [19]. Both point the same way — the compartments are not interchangeable — but they do so from opposite ends, and neither validates fecal concentration as a production proxy.

3. **Ex vivo culture is a third compartment again.** None of [1–6, 18, 19] studied closed *ex vivo* culture. Our 0→48 h culture endpoint is *neither* fecal SCFA in vivo *nor* flux: it is net accumulation in a closed vessel with no absorptive sink. Because the colonic epithelium continuously removes butyrate in vivo [2, 4], a closed vessel will over-represent butyrate accumulation relative to the in vivo lumen. This asymmetry cuts in our favour for caution and should be stated in 4.3.

4. **Acetate is doubly ambiguous.** Acetate is simultaneously the most abundant product and a consumed substrate for butyrogenesis [9, 2, 11]. Net acetate accumulation is therefore the *least* interpretable of our three endpoints as a "production" quantity — arguably more so than butyrate. Worth an explicit sentence.

5. **Sakata's caveat cuts at our design too.** Sakata [1] warns that results without dose–response and time-course data can be "seriously misleading," and cautions specifically against over-emphasis on butyrate. Our design has a time-course only insofar as 0 and 48 h are both measured; we should not present 48 h net accumulation as though it characterised fermentation rate. Citing [1] obliges us to apply it to ourselves.

6. **Taxon-to-function inference is not licensed.** [10] shows butyrate and propionate capacity usually sit in different species and that some taxa switch products by substrate; [8] shows butyrate producers are phylogenetically scattered; [2] found faecal butyrogenic capacity did not predict actual acetate→butyrate conversion. Together these forbid inferring SCFA function from 16S composition. **This is directly relevant to the claim-lock on *Fusicatenibacter*.**

## What we must not overclaim (given claim lock)

- **Do not** write "SCFA production" for a 0→48 h culture endpoint. Write "net accumulation" or "net output." [1, 5, 6]
- **Do not** infer colonic delivery or host systemic exposure from culture SCFA. Systemic availability is low and SCFA-specific (butyrate ≈2%) [2], and the assay has no absorptive sink [2, 4].
- **Do not** infer glycemic, insulin-sensitivity, appetite, or obesity-treatment benefit. Our design has no host, no absorption, no endocrine axis; the human evidence base for these effects is itself hedged [16, 17].
- **Do not** frame greater net SCFA accumulation as intrinsically favourable. Higher fecal SCFA has been associated with adverse cardiometabolic phenotypes [18].
- **Do not** claim SDC superiority over RDC for any SCFA. No citation here supports it, and [20] shows endpoint values conflate rate and extent, so an endpoint difference would not establish a fermentation-rate difference either.
- **Do not** name *Fusicatenibacter* (or any taxon) as the butyrate source or as an SDC utilizer. No retrieved source assigns butyrogenic function to *Fusicatenibacter*; [10, 8, 2] specifically caution against composition→function inference. Butyrate is frequently a **secondary** product of cross-feeding [7, 11], so a correlated taxon need not be the producer.
- **Do not** claim obesity-group equivalence or "preserved fermentation capacity." Nothing retrieved supports equivalence testing, and no source frames a null culture difference as preserved in vivo capacity.
- **Do not** describe 48 h net accumulation as "fermentation rate," "fermentation kinetics," or "fermentation capacity." [20, 13, 1]

**SECTION:** Introduction, paragraph 2 — fermentation biology setup

**PRIOR CLAIM:** Nondigested carbohydrates entering the large intestine are fermented by the resident microbiota to acetate, propionate and butyrate through pathways distributed across phylogenetically distinct taxa, with butyrate frequently arising as a secondary product of cross-feeding on intermediates released by primary degraders.

**CITATIONS (max 3 lead + 2 backup):** Lead — Louis & Flint 2017 [7]; Reichardt et al. 2014 [10]; Belenguer et al. 2006 [11]. Backup — Louis & Flint 2009 [8]; Louis et al. 2022 [12].

**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** Butyrate and propionate capacities generally reside in different species [10]; a few taxa switch products by substrate [10]. Cross-feeding proceeds by both end-product consumption and partial-breakdown-product transfer [11].

**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Carbohydrate that escapes host digestion is fermented by the colonic microbiota to the short-chain fatty acids acetate, propionate and butyrate, via pathways that are unevenly distributed across taxa; butyrate in particular is frequently a secondary product, formed by organisms that consume lactate, succinate or acetate released by primary polysaccharide degraders rather than by degrading the substrate themselves."

**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** **Supported.** Standard physiology, no taxon-specific or group-specific inference, no benefit claim.

**SECTION:** Introduction, paragraph 2 — why we say "net accumulation"

**PRIOR CLAIM:** Measured SCFA concentrations reflect the balance of production and consumption, not production rate or flux; fecal and luminal concentrations in particular are not valid proxies for production.

**CITATIONS (max 3 lead + 2 backup):** Lead — Sakata 2019 [1]; Kirschner et al. 2025 [3]; Duncan et al. 2002 [9]. Backup — Verbeke et al. 2015 [6]; den Besten et al. 2013 [5].

**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** Acetate is the clearest case, being both a major product and a consumed substrate for butyrogenesis [9, 11]. Sakata's caution applies to our own endpoint as well as to fecal measures [1].

**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Because several abundant butyrate-producing taxa consume acetate while forming butyrate, and because SCFAs are continuously turned over within a fermenting community, a measured SCFA concentration represents net accumulation — production minus consumption and interconversion — rather than production rate or metabolic flux; accordingly we report net accumulation over the 0–48 h incubation throughout."

**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** **Supported.** This is the guardrail sentence itself; it constrains rather than extends claims.

**SECTION:** Discussion 4.3 — limits of the ex vivo endpoint

**PRIOR CLAIM:** Net SCFA accumulation in a closed 48 h culture is a measure of substrate fermentation potential of the donor community under defined conditions; it does not index colonic delivery, host absorption, or systemic SCFA exposure.

**CITATIONS (max 3 lead + 2 backup):** Lead — Boets et al. 2017 [2]; Cummings et al. 1987 [4]; Sakata 2019 [1]. Backup — Verbeke et al. 2015 [6]; Oliver et al. 2024 [19].

**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** The absence of an absorptive sink is not a neutral simplification — it is directionally biasing. In vivo, butyrate is preferentially removed by colonocytes and propionate by the liver [4], and only ~2% of colonic butyrate reaches the circulation [2]. A closed vessel therefore over-represents butyrate accumulation relative to the in vivo lumen.

**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Our incubations are closed systems without an absorptive epithelium, whereas in vivo the colonic mucosa preferentially removes butyrate and the liver extracts propionate, such that only a small and SCFA-specific fraction of colonic short-chain fatty acids reaches the systemic circulation; net accumulation measured here therefore characterises the fermentative output of the donor community under defined ex vivo conditions and cannot be read as colonic delivery or host exposure."

**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** **Supported.** Explicitly limiting. Blocks glycemic/clinical extrapolation.

**SECTION:** Discussion 4.3 — do not read higher SCFA as better

**PRIOR CLAIM:** Greater net SCFA accumulation cannot be interpreted as a favourable phenotype; in human cohorts higher fecal SCFA has been associated with adverse rather than favourable cardiometabolic profiles.

**CITATIONS (max 3 lead + 2 backup):** Lead — de la Cuesta-Zuluaga et al. 2018 [18]; Canfora et al. 2015 [16]; Verbeke et al. 2015 [6]. Backup — Canfora et al. 2019 [17]; Oliver et al. 2024 [19].

**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** This directly opposes the prevailing mechanistic framing [15, 16, 17]. Both readings should be presented; the association in [18] is observational and its authors interpret it as reduced absorption efficiency, which is a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism.

**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Although short-chain fatty acids are widely described as beneficial mediators of host metabolism, higher faecal short-chain fatty acid concentrations have been associated in large human cohorts with lower microbial diversity and less favourable cardiometabolic profiles — possibly reflecting reduced absorption — so a larger net accumulation in vitro should not be equated with a more favourable functional phenotype."

**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** **Supported.** Actively blocks a non-goal (clinical/glycemic benefit) and the "more is better" reading.

**SECTION:** Discussion 4.3 — 48 h endpoint and fermentation kinetics

**PRIOR CLAIM:** A single 48 h endpoint integrates the whole incubation and cannot distinguish rate from extent of fermentation; substrates with comparable endpoint SCFA may follow markedly different time courses.

**CITATIONS (max 3 lead + 2 backup):** Lead — Kaur et al. 2011 [20]; Sakata 2019 [1]. Backup — Muñoz-Tamayo et al. 2011 [13]; Duncan et al. 2009 [14].

**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** 48 h is a conventional batch window [20], which supports comparability with prior work but does not make the endpoint kinetically interpretable. Accumulating acid also lowers pH within a closed vessel, and pH shifts independently reshape which taxa thrive [14] — a property of the assay as much as of the substrate. This is particularly pointed given that our formulations are defined by *digestion* rate, which is not the same construct as *fermentation* rate.

**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Net accumulation measured at a single 48 h endpoint integrates the entire incubation, and in vitro comparisons of dietary substrates have shown that materials reaching similar 48 h short-chain fatty acid values can differ substantially in when during the window fermentation occurs; our design therefore does not resolve fermentation rate or timing, and accumulating fermentation acids progressively acidify the closed system in a way that can itself influence community composition."

**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** **Supported.** Limiting; also pre-empts any implicit slide from "slow-digesting carbohydrate" to "slow-fermenting substrate."

**SECTION:** Discussion 4.3 — composition does not license functional attribution

**PRIOR CLAIM:** Taxonomic composition does not license attribution of SCFA output to specific taxa, because butyrogenic capacity is phylogenetically scattered, frequently secondary, and poorly predicted by faecal community measures.

**CITATIONS (max 3 lead + 2 backup):** Lead — Boets et al. 2017 [2]; Reichardt et al. 2014 [10]; Louis & Flint 2009 [8]. Backup — Louis & Flint 2017 [7]; Belenguer et al. 2006 [11].

**CONFLICTS / NUANCE:** Boets et al. found colonic acetate→butyrate conversion was **not** related to the butyrate-producing capacity measured in the corresponding faecal samples [2] — a direct empirical failure of the composition→function inference in humans.

**MANUSCRIPT-SAFE SENTENCE:** "Butyrate-forming capacity is distributed across phylogenetically diverse taxa and often depends on cross-feeding rather than direct substrate degradation, and in humans the extent of colonic acetate-to-butyrate conversion was unrelated to butyrate-producing capacity measured in the corresponding faecal sample; we therefore treat associations between community composition and short-chain fatty acid net accumulation as exploratory and do not attribute short-chain fatty acid output to individual taxa."

**CLAIM-LOCK CHECK:** **Supported as written (exploratory framing).** Would become **unsupported** if used to name *Fusicatenibacter* — or any genus — as an SDC utilizer or the butyrate source. Retrieved literature provides no basis for that attribution.

## Language pitfalls to avoid

1. **"Production" / "produced" for a measured concentration or endpoint.** This is the central pitfall, and Sakata [1] names it explicitly: fecal and luminal concentrations "should not be used as measures of SCFA production or absorption." Use **net accumulation**, **net output**, or **net change over 0–48 h**. Note that much of the source literature — including titles we cite [20] — uses "production" loosely for exactly this quantity; we should not inherit the usage just because we cite the paper.

2. **"Fermentation capacity," "fermentation rate," or "fermentation kinetics" for a single endpoint.** A 0→48 h difference is an integral, not a rate. Substrates with equal 48 h values can ferment at entirely different times within the window [20]. Prefer "net accumulation over 48 h under the incubation conditions used." Reserve "capacity" for cases where a rate or maximum was actually measured.

3. **"Delivered to the colon" / "available to the host" / "SCFA exposure" from culture data.** Systemic availability is low and strongly SCFA-specific (~36% acetate, ~9% propionate, ~2% butyrate) [2], and butyrate is preferentially consumed by the epithelium before it ever reaches portal blood [4]. Our closed system has no absorptive sink at all, so it cannot speak to delivery or exposure in either direction.

4. **"Beneficial," "favourable," "improved," or "enhanced" attached to a higher SCFA value.** Higher *fecal* SCFA has been associated with dysbiosis, obesity and hypertension [18], and the human evidence for metabolic benefit remains hedged even in advocacy-leaning reviews [16, 17]. Use directional, non-evaluative language: "greater net accumulation," "higher net butyrate output." Never "improved butyrate production."

5. **Naming a taxon as "the producer" or "a butyrate producer" on the basis of a correlation.** Butyrate is often a secondary product formed by cross-feeders that do not degrade the substrate [7, 11], butyrogenic capacity is phylogenetically scattered [8, 10], and faecal butyrogenic capacity failed to predict actual in vivo conversion [2]. A correlated genus may be a primary degrader, a cross-feeder, a bystander, or a consequence of pH change [14]. This applies with full force to *Fusicatenibacter*.

6. **Sliding from "slow-digesting" to "slow-fermenting."** These are different constructs measured by different assays. Our formulations are defined by digestion rate; nothing in our endpoint measures fermentation rate [20]. Keep the two vocabularies separate throughout.
